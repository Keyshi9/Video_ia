# Revolution short - script candidates

## 1. The Bastille: Stormed for Gunpowder, Not Prisoners (100 words)

### Facts and sources
- The Bastille held only seven prisoners when stormed on 14 July 1789: four forgers, two mentally ill men, and a count held at his family's request (Britannica's grouping). (https://www.britannica.com/event/storming-of-the-Bastille)
- That morning the crowd took about 29,000-32,000 muskets from the Invalides, with no powder or shot. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storming_of_the_Bastille)
- About 250 barrels of gunpowder had been moved into the Bastille a few days earlier for safekeeping, which made it the target. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storming_of_the_Bastille)
- Fighting began about 1:30 pm; de Launay ordered a ceasefire about 5:00 pm and the fortress fell about 5:30 pm, so roughly four hours. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storming_of_the_Bastille)
- Once freed, the prisoners were paraded through Paris in triumph. (Schama, Citizens (1989); Lüsebrink & Reichardt, The Bastille (1997))
- Sade had been transferred out of the Bastille in early July 1789 (about ten days before) after shouting to passers-by that the prisoners were being massacred. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storming_of_the_Bastille ; https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marquis-de-Sade)

### Script
The most famous jail ever stormed was really a gunpowder run.
Paris, July fourteenth, 1789.
That morning, the crowd grabbed about thirty thousand muskets. No powder.
The powder? Two hundred fifty barrels, moved into the Bastille days before. For safekeeping.
About four hours of shooting later, the governor gave up.
Luckily, the prisoners were freed.
Unluckily, there were seven. Four forgers, two madmen, and a count locked up by his own family.
The crowd paraded them, then went back to the barrels.
The Marquis de Sade missed it. He'd yelled to passers-by that prisoners were being massacred, so they'd moved him out.

### Beats
- **Hook**: "The most famous jail ever stormed was really a gunpowder run." | Late afternoon, low angle on the grey stone fortress gate. A cheering crowd of bean-heads in phrygian caps and tricornes marches out hugging powder barrels like trophies. They walk straight past seven small, blinking prisoners in rags at the edge of the frame without looking at them.
- **Setting**: "Paris, July fourteenth, 1789." | Slow crane over the storybook town in morning light: river, stone bridge, tiled roofs, cockades on hats, a few flags. The Bastille's eight towers loom at the edge of town. No Sade in this shot.
- **Action 1**: "That morning, the crowd grabbed about thirty thousand muskets. No powder." | In front of a single domed facade (the Invalides), the crowd raises a forest of muskets in triumph. One man tips his powder pouch upside down and nothing falls out. Arms slowly lower all along the crowd.
- **Action 2**: "The powder? Two hundred fifty barrels, moved into the Bastille days before. For safekeeping." | Sepia-tinted flashback: soldiers push a two-wheeled handcart stacked with barrels over the bridge, coming from a different street (not the domed building), and through the fortress gate, which shuts. Cut back to colour: the whole crowd at the dome turns its heads as one toward the distant towers.
- **Action 3 / Snap**: "About four hours of shooting later, the governor gave up." | The crowd fills the square before the fortress. Puffs of cartoon smoke drift from the towers (no physics, no injuries). Shadows swing from midday to late afternoon, with the sun still high. A white cloth flutters from a tower and the drawbridge lowers.
- **Luckily**: "Luckily, the prisoners were freed." | The crowd pours through the gate with lanterns and caps flying, arms open toward the dark dungeon doorways.
- **Unluckily**: "Unluckily, there were seven. Four forgers, two madmen, and a count locked up by his own family." | Seven bewildered bean-heads shuffle out. Four are inky-fingered and drop fake coins; two wander off in odd directions; one in a powdered wig straightens his cuffs, glancing nervously at an imagined family portrait he holds. The vast courtyard dwarfs them.
- **Explanation**: "The crowd paraded them, then went back to the barrels." | The seven are hoisted onto shoulders and carried in a quick loop around the square, then set down gently. Behind them everyone forms a human chain rolling powder barrels out of the gate, hand to hand.
- **Callback twist**: "The Marquis de Sade missed it. He'd yelled to passers-by that prisoners were being massacred, so they'd moved him out." | Sepia flashback again: a wigged bean-head at a high barred window, hands cupped around his mouth, shouting at startled passers-by below. Guards walk him out of the gate on foot and away over the bridge. Back to colour: the same window is now empty while the roaring crowd fills the square below. Hold on the empty window.

## 2. The King Caught by His Own Money (Flight to Varennes, 1791) (100 words)

### Facts and sources
- On the night of 20-21 June 1791 Louis XVI and his family fled Paris, the King disguised as a valet ('Durand'). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_to_Varennes ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Drouet_(revolutionary))
- The family travelled in one heavy coach rather than splitting into lighter carriages. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_to_Varennes)
- The Queen got lost in the dark, costing about half an hour; later a wheel struck a post and the traces had to be repaired. (https://www.worldhistory.org/Flight_to_Varennes/)
- Postmaster Drouet of Sainte-Menehould said he recognised the King from his portrait on an assignat (his own account). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Drouet_(revolutionary))
- Drouet rode by a shorter route and reached Varennes just after the coach. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Drouet_(revolutionary) ; https://www.worldhistory.org/Flight_to_Varennes/)
- The relay of fresh horses was waiting in the lower town across the river; the royal party lost time searching for it in the upper town. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_to_Varennes ; Tackett, When the King Took Flight (2003))
- Locals blocked the bridge over the Aire with a cart, and the family was detained at Varennes. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Drouet_(revolutionary))
- Napoleon is said to have told Drouet he had changed the face of the world (an anecdote, not firmly documented). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Drouet_(revolutionary) ; maitron.fr (Drouet entry, calls it a legend))

### Script
The King of France was caught, the story goes, by his own money.
June 1791. Louis the Sixteenth slips out of Paris as a valet.
The family won't split up: one huge, slow coach.
The Queen gets lost. Half an hour.
A wheel hits a post. More delay.
At Sainte-Menehould, the postmaster checks a banknote. Same face, he later claimed.
He takes a shortcut.
Luckily, fresh horses wait at Varennes. Unluckily, at the other end of town.
Townspeople block the bridge. Arrested.
Napoleon reportedly told him: you changed the face of the world. Starting with the one on the money.

### Beats
- **Hook**: "The King of France was caught, the story goes, by his own money." | Close on a paper banknote drifting down through a night sky. A large, centred bean-head portrait with a crown fills it, readable in under a second. It lands on dark cobbles; a huge coach wheel rolls past it and the camera tilts up to follow.
- **Setting**: "June 1791. Louis the Sixteenth slips out of Paris as a valet." | Night street with lanterns and a palace silhouette. A round bean-head in a plain brown coat and cheap round hat, with a curl of royal wig poking out, tiptoes from a side gate and looks left and right.
- **Action 1**: "The family won't split up: one huge, slow coach." | Side view of one oversized coach, horses cropped out of frame. Family faces squeezed into every window. A second, small light carriage waits empty beside it and slowly rolls away unused.
- **Action 2a**: "The Queen gets lost. Half an hour." | A bean-head woman in a hooded cloak with a lantern walks the wrong way down a dark alley, stops, turns back. Inside the coach the king taps his foot while a candle melts down fast.
- **Action 2b**: "A wheel hits a post. More delay." | Daylight country road. The wheel bumps a stone post, the coach tilts with a cartoon bounce, a strap pops into frame. Two coachmen tie an enormous knot while the sun visibly slides across the sky.
- **Snap**: "At Sainte-Menehould, the postmaster checks a banknote. Same face, he later claimed." | Sunny village square. The coach pulls away. Behind it a lean postmaster in a waistcoat pulls a banknote from his pocket, looks at it, looks up at the departing coach, back at the note. His eyes widen.
- **Action 3**: "He takes a shortcut." | Wide landscape: the slow coach crawls along the main road on one side of the frame. On the other side, through a forest, only a dragoon-style helmet bobs above the treetops and a trail of dust cuts across on a separate path. No rider animated.
- **Deadpan double**: "Luckily, fresh horses wait at Varennes. Unluckily, at the other end of town." | Night, high shot of a small town split by a river. Upper town: the coach stops, people step out with lanterns, peering around. Camera pans across the bridge to the lower town, where a pair of fresh horses stands calmly waiting, unseen.
- **Consequence**: "Townspeople block the bridge. Arrested." | Townspeople in nightcaps roll a furniture cart onto the bridge and pile barrels on it; National Guards in tricornes arrive behind them. The coach halts. Camera pulls up high: a stone fortress glows far off on the horizon, out of reach.
- **Callback twist**: "Napoleon reportedly told him: you changed the face of the world. Starting with the one on the money." | No new emperor model: back on the cobbles from the opening, the same banknote tumbles along the street, years later. It flips over and now shows a red phrygian cap where the king's face used to be.

## 3. The Fortress Sold as Souvenirs (98 words)

### Facts and sources
- Builder Pierre-François Palloy ran the demolition of the Bastille from mid-July 1789 (official authorisation 16 July; he claimed to have started on the evening of the 14th). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Fran%C3%A7ois_Palloy ; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Fran%C3%A7ois_Palloy)
- About 1,000 workers took part; demolition ran until about May 1791. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Fran%C3%A7ois_Palloy)
- His staff ran paid tours of the dungeons, displaying skeletons/bones. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Fran%C3%A7ois_Palloy)
- He made Bastille models from its stones (first carved, later cast from stone powder and mortar) and offered one to each of the 83 departments, asking only to be repaid for transport and postage. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Fran%C3%A7ois_Palloy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_models_of_the_Bastille)
- In early 1794 he was jailed about two months on charges of embezzlement and of being a Hébertist. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Fran%C3%A7ois_Palloy)
- Bastille stones were reused in the Pont de la Concorde, among other sites. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Fran%C3%A7ois_Palloy ; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pont_de_la_Concorde)

### Script
One man sold the Bastille, piece by piece.
July 1789. Within days, a builder named Palloy is tearing it down.
About a thousand workers, nearly two years. Every stone, a souvenir.
Paid dungeon tours, with bones found below.
He makes mini-Bastilles from its stones and offers one to every department. All eighty-three.
Luckily, he asks France only for the postage.
Unluckily, in 1794, he's jailed anyway: accused of embezzlement, and of backing the wrong radicals.
Two months in a cell.
Many of its stones went into a bridge over the Seine. Paris still walks over pieces of the Bastille.

### Beats
- **Hook**: "One man sold the Bastille, piece by piece." | The stone fortress stands in Paris. A single block lifts out of a tower like a drawer, shrinks into a neat wrapped parcel and lands in a bean-head builder's open palm. He grins and pockets a coin.
- **Setting**: "July 1789. Within days, a builder named Palloy is tearing it down." | Day after the storming: flags and trampled caps in the square. The builder in tricorne and work apron climbs a ladder against a tower and prises off the top block with a small crowbar.
- **Action 1**: "About a thousand workers, nearly two years. Every stone, a souvenir." | Time-lapse: a long human chain passes blocks down the street while seasons flip (snow, blossom, sun) and the towers drop layer by layer. At the end of the chain, small stones go into display boxes on a stall.
- **Action 2**: "Paid dungeon tours, with bones found below." | At a low doorway in the half-demolished fortress, a staff member takes coins from a queue of wigged visitors. Inside, by lantern light, a cartoon skeleton lies in the dirt; a visitor's wig pops straight up in fright.
- **Action 3 (escalation)**: "He makes mini-Bastilles from its stones and offers one to every department. All eighty-three." | A big block is sliced into cubes that pop into miniature fortresses. Now the full storybook map of France: couriers push handcarts and barges float down the river, each carrying a mini-Bastille outward to towns in every direction.
- **Luckily**: "Luckily, he asks France only for the postage." | Palloy hands a clerk a tiny bill; a single coin slides back across the desk. He bows grandly.
- **Unluckily**: "Unluckily, in 1794, he's jailed anyway: accused of embezzlement, and of backing the wrong radicals." | A stern official in a wig slaps a tall stack of papers onto the same desk. Two guards step in on either side of Palloy; he gulps.
- **Consequence**: "Two months in a cell." | Palloy on a stool in a small cell; through the barred window the sky flips day-night-day rapidly. On the shelf beside him sits one little mini-Bastille.
- **Callback twist**: "Many of its stones went into a bridge over the Seine. Paris still walks over pieces of the Bastille." | The leftover pile of fortress blocks by the river lifts and arcs, block by block, into a stone bridge. Camera rises over the years: walkers in modern clothes stroll across it. One parapet stone briefly glows with the fortress silhouette.

## Notes
Ranking reasoning: Bastille comes first because it is the best-sourced and has the clearest physical cause and effect (barrels go in, so the crowd goes in). Varennes is second: the cause-and-effect chain is strong, but the central assignat moment has to be hedged. Palloy is third: it is charming, but several of its key claims rest on a single Wikipedia page. Word counts are written words. Spoken numbers add about 2-4 units, so each script runs about 26-28 s.

1) BASTILLE. Estimated score 8/10.
Changes:
- The hook no longer says "prison break" ("most famous jail ever stormed... gunpowder run"), and the number seven is kept back for the Unluckily beat.
- "A day of fighting" became "About four hours of shooting", and the picture goes from midday to late afternoon, not to dusk.
- "Nobody came for them" and the fading-cheer picture are gone. The crowd now parades the prisoners and then goes back to the barrels, which matches the record.
- The prisoner list is now "two madmen, a count locked up by his own family".
- For Sade: "one famous inmate" is dropped, and so are the waste-funnel megaphone (poorly sourced) and the plant in the 14 July window (wrong chronology). He now appears only in a sepia flashback, walked out on foot, with no carriage or horses.
- The barrel handcart comes from a different street, not the Invalides, because the powder came from the Arsenal.
Caveats:
- Sources list the seven prisoners differently. Wikipedia says Whyte, not Solages, was held at his family's request, though Solages was held under his father's lettre de cachet. The line follows Britannica.
- The fighting lasted about 3.5-4 h.
- Sade's transfer date varies between 2 and 4 July, so the script gives no date.
- The parade of the freed prisoners comes from secondary histories (Schama), not from the Wikipedia page.
- The crowd also wanted the cannons withdrawn, so "gunpowder run" simplifies its motive.

2) VARENNES. Estimated score 7/10.
Changes:
- The hook is hedged ("the story goes"), and the snap is "he later claimed". The picture shows Drouet checking the note after the coach has left, not holding it up to the King's face.
- "At dawn" and the exact repair time are dropped ("More delay").
- "Mister Durand" is cut.
- The tense is present throughout the middle.
- The weak "head start / ex-dragoon" pair became the true relay irony: the fresh horses were waiting at the other end of town.
- Drouet takes a separate shortcut and never overtakes the coach. Only his helmet and dust are shown, with no rider animated.
- Townspeople block the bridge first and the Guard arrives after.
- The Napoleon line is marked "reportedly". The medal ceremony and the emperor model are cut, and the line plays over the banknote callback, set "years later".
- Horses are cropped out of almost every shot.
Caveats:
- The assignat recognition is Drouet's own account.
- The Napoleon quote is called a legend by the Maitron dictionary.
- The phrygian-cap banknote stands for the Republican assignats of 1792-93, not a direct result of the arrest.
- The flight's failure also came from Choiseul's hussars leaving and from broken relay links, so the hook is a deliberate, hedged simplification.

3) PALLOY. Estimated score 6.5/10.
Changes:
- "Mailed" is gone. The hook is now "sold the Bastille, piece by piece", and the full map shot is kept for Action 3, so the escalation no longer repeats the hook.
- "That same evening" became "Within days".
- The demolition time is added ("nearly two years").
- The tours now show bones found on site rather than "props", and the staff run them.
- The models are "made from its stones" (covers both carved and cast) and "offered" to the departments rather than confirmed delivered.
- The arrest now names both charges (embezzlement and backing the Hébertists, "the wrong radicals").
- The repeated door-clang cell shot is cut.
- The bridge line is now "many of its stones... pieces of the Bastille".
- Horses are replaced by handcarts and barges.
Caveats:
- Three claims rest mainly on English Wikipedia: postage only, two months in jail, and skeleton tours. They need a second source (Babelon, Carnavalet) before recording.
- Placing "only asked for postage" next to "jailed anyway" is comic irony. No source links the charge to the models.
- The Pont de la Concorde was widened in 1930-32, so only part of what people walk on is original Bastille stone.
